# Part 1. General Information

## 1. Purpose

Spectrum for Living supports about 1,000 people across 14 group homes, 5 supervised apartments, 5 adult day programs, and an intermediate care facility in Closter. Homes operate 24 hours a day. When a phone, a network, or a computer system stops working, care documentation, medication records, billing, and emergency contact are all affected at once.

This manual does four things:

1. Protects the private information of the people we support, our employees, our donors, and our vendors.
2. Keeps offices, homes, day programs, and vehicles running when technology fails.
3. Tells staff exactly how to get help and how to report a problem.
4. Gives leadership a regular set of numbers to review so that risks are visible before they become incidents.

## 2. Scope

This manual applies to every person who uses a Spectrum for Living account, device, network, or system. That includes full time and part time employees, Direct Support Professionals, nurses, supervisors, managers, directors, executives, per diem and temporary staff, interns, volunteers, contractors, consultants, vendors with system access, and board members.

It covers all locations in Bergen, Passaic, and Middlesex counties, all administrative offices, all residential and day program sites, the Closter ICF, maintenance and fleet locations, and any place where staff work remotely.

It covers all Spectrum for Living equipment and all personal devices used for agency work, including computers, tablets, phones, printers, copiers, desk phones, mobile hotspots, and network equipment.

Some subjects are handled by other departments and are not covered here. Employee relations matters go to Human Resources. Incidents involving a person we support follow the Unusual Incident Report process. Clinical questions follow the clinical chain of command. Life safety emergencies go to 911.

1. Use your Spectrum for Living account for agency work.

## 6. Requesting an Exception

An exception is written permission to do something differently for a set period of time. Anything done differently without written permission is a violation.

1. Complete Form I.
2. State the rule you cannot follow and the operating reason.
3. Describe what you will do instead to reduce the risk.
4. Send the form to the Director of Information Technology.
5. Approval depends on the level of risk.

| Risk level | Approved by | Maximum length |
|---|---|---|
| Low, such as a non standard accessory | Director of Information Technology | 12 months |
| Moderate, such as delaying a computer replacement | Chief Operating Officer | 6 months |
| High, involving 2-Step Verification, encryption, backups, PHI, or payments | Chief Operating Officer and VP Compliance | 90 days |
| Critical, affecting HIPAA, CMS, DDD, or insurance requirements | Chief Executive Officer and legal counsel | 30 days |

The following are never approved:

1. Sharing a password.
2. Turning off 2-Step Verification on an account that reaches email, health records, finance, payroll, or Human Resources systems.
3. Resetting a password based only on an email request.
4. Changing vendor banking information without a verification phone call.
5. Entering information about a person we support into an AI tool.
6. Storing confidential information on a device that is not encrypted.

Staff will not be disciplined for promptly reporting a mistake they made. Hiding a mistake is the serious problem, not making one.

| Situation | Typical response |
|---|---|
| Honest mistake, reported right away | Coaching and refresher training |
| Repeating the same mistake after coaching | Documented counseling by the supervisor |
| Looking at records without a work reason | Investigation by Human Resources and Compliance, possible termination |
| Sharing a password or working around a security control | Access review and documented discipline |
| Hiding an incident | Discipline up to termination |

## IT-01. Email Use

| Owner | Director of Information Technology |
|---|---|
| Applies to | Everyone with a Spectrum for Living email account |
| Systems | Gmail, Google Drive, Google Vault |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Keep email professional, keep private information out of the wrong hands, and keep agency records where they can be found.

**Procedure**

1. Use your Spectrum for Living account for all agency business. Do not use a personal email account.
2. Do not forward agency email to a personal account. Automatic forwarding to an outside address is turned off and is not permitted.
3. Check email at least once per assigned shift. Supervisors, managers, and directors check twice a day.
4. Answer internal email within one business day. Answer families, funders, and regulators the same business day when possible.
5. Write a subject line that states the topic and what you need. Example: "Action needed: October fire drill schedule, reply by 10/3."
6. Put your request in the first two sentences.
7. Use Reply All only when every person on the list needs the information. Use BCC only to protect the privacy of a group of recipients, never to copy someone secretly.
8. Check every address before you send. Gmail address suggestions are the most common cause of email sent to the wrong person.
9. Do not put a person's name together with a diagnosis, a service detail, a Social Security number, or an account number in a subject line.
10. Do not attach files with protected health information to an email going outside the agency. Share a Google Drive link with named recipients instead, or use the secure method listed in SOP IT-19.
11. Send a link rather than an attachment whenever you can. A link can be turned off later. An attachment cannot.
12. Do not delete email that documents a decision, a service, an incident, a payment, or a conversation with a regulator, funder, donor, or vendor.
13. Set an out of office reply for any absence longer than one business day. Name a covering coworker. Do not say that you are traveling or out of the country.
14. Report any suspicious message within one hour using the steps in SOP IT-08.
15. Forward media questions to Marketing and the Chief Executive Officer without answering. Forward legal papers, subpoenas, and attorney letters to legal counsel and Compliance the same day. Forward DDD, Department of Health, and Medicaid correspondence to the VP Compliance and the Chief Operating Officer the same day.
16. Never act on an emailed request to change payment or banking information. Follow SOP IT-10.

**If you send email to the wrong person**

1. Do not delete the message. It is the record of what happened.
2. Tell your supervisor right away.
3. Report it to IT and Compliance within one hour.
4. Ask the person who received it to delete it and write down their answer.
5. Complete Form E. Compliance decides whether the event must be reported to a regulator. That decision is not made by the sender.

**Escalate.** Suspected mailbox break in, protected health information sent to the wrong person, or any message asking for money or employee data.

**Records.** Email is kept for [7 years, to be confirmed by legal counsel]. Reports of misdirected email are kept for 6 years.

**Response time.** New mailbox set up one business day before the start date. Reported phishing reviewed within four business hours.

## IT-02. Password Help, Account Lockout, and 2-Step Verification

| Owner | Director of Information Technology |
|---|---|
| Applies to | All staff and all systems |
| Systems | Google Workspace and all agency applications |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Get people back into their accounts quickly without creating a way for an outsider to take an account over.

**Rule that cannot be waived.** A password is never reset based only on an email, a text, or a chat message. Identity is always confirmed by live phone or video. This applies to every account at every level, including executives.

**How to get help**

1. Try the self service reset at [insert link], if available for your account.
2. If that does not work, call [insert IT phone]. After hours, call [insert emergency IT number].
3. Have ready your full name, job title, department, site, supervisor's name, the system involved, and the exact error message.
4. Expect to be asked questions to confirm who you are. If IT cannot confirm your identity, the password will not be reset. That is the control working correctly.
5. You will receive a temporary password by phone or to the number already on file. You will not receive it by email.
6. Sign in before the temporary password expires and set a new one.
7. Confirm to IT that you have access so the ticket can be closed.

**Password rules**

1. Use a passphrase of at least 15 characters. Several unrelated words are stronger than a short complex string.
2. Use a different password for every system.
3. Never share a password with anyone, including IT, your supervisor, and executives. IT will never ask for your password.
4. If a coworker asks for your password, say no and tell your supervisor.
5. If several people need the same mailbox or file, ask IT for shared access. Do not hand out your own sign in.

**If you are locked out**

1. Wait 15 to 30 minutes. Many lockouts clear on their own.
2. Check that Caps Lock is off.
3. Check your other devices. An old saved password on a phone or tablet retrying in the background is the most common cause.
4. If it does not clear, call [insert IT phone].

**If you lose the phone with your 2-Step Verification app**

1. Call [insert IT phone] right away, day or night. Do not wait for your next shift.
2. Tell IT if the phone also had agency email or files on it.
3. IT will confirm your identity, remove the lost device, and help you set up the new one.
4. Keep your backup codes in a locked place, not in email and not on the same phone.
5. Before you trade in or erase an old phone, submit a ticket so 2-Step Verification can be moved first.

2-Step Verification is never turned off to get someone back into an account. Access is restored by setting it up again.

**Escalate.** Any caller who pressures IT to skip the identity check, any reset for an executive or administrator account, any sign that an account has been taken over.

**Records.** Every reset has a ticket showing who was verified, how, by whom, and when. Kept 7 years.

**Response time.** Business hours 30 minutes. Lockout affecting care documentation or a payroll deadline, 15 minutes, any hour.

## IT-04. Printer Problems and Printing Rules

| Owner | Director of Information Technology |
|---|---|
| Applies to | All staff who print or scan |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Solve most printing problems at the desk, prevent injuries, and keep printed pages with private information from being left out.

**Try these steps first**

1. Read the message on the printer screen. Write it down and take a photo.
2. If the screen is dark, check the power switch and both ends of the power cord.
3. Open the paper tray. Confirm the paper is squared, below the fill line, and not damp. Fan the stack, reload it, and close the tray until it clicks.
4. Look for a toner message. Replace the toner only if you have been trained and a spare is on site.
5. Check that the network cable is pushed in at the printer and at the wall. Do not change any settings.
6. Open the print queue on your computer, cancel any stuck jobs, and print one test page.
7. Check that you selected the right printer. Printing to the wrong device is the most common report of a broken printer.
8. Turn the printer off, wait 30 seconds, turn it on, and wait up to three minutes for it to finish starting.
9. If the problem is only on your computer, restart your computer.
10. If it still does not work, submit an IT ticket describing what you saw and what you tried.

**Paper jams.** Read the screen for the jam location. Open only the doors the screen or the diagram shows. Pull the paper slowly in the direction it was traveling, never backward. Remove every piece, including small torn corners. Close all doors firmly. If the jam message returns after two tries, stop and submit a ticket.

**Do not.** Open internal panels or reach past the paper and toner areas, because parts inside are hot enough to burn. Use tools or force to remove paper. Attempt repairs on leased or contracted equipment. Install printer software from the internet. Move a printer without a ticket. Connect a personal printer to an agency computer or network.

**Printing private information**

1. Print only to a device you can reach right away. Never print confidential material to another floor or another site.
2. Stand at the printer when printing anything with health, personnel, or financial information.
3. Collect your pages immediately. Do not leave them to pick up later.
4. If you find someone else's confidential pages, give them to the owner or your supervisor, or put them in the locked shredding bin.
5. Shred confidential paper in a locked container or a cross cut shredder. Never put it in trash or recycling.
6. If confidential pages printed to the wrong location and cannot be recovered, tell your supervisor, report it to IT and Compliance within one hour, and complete Form E.

**Call right away** if there is smoke, a burning smell, sparking, or an electrical shock. Unplug the device if it is safe to do so, and call 911 if there is smoke or fire.

**Supplies.** Each site keeps one spare toner per installed cartridge. Reorder when you take the last spare, not when you run out. Order only through [insert process].

**Records.** Ticket history and service cost by device are kept for the life of the device plus 7 years. A printer with three or more tickets in 90 days goes to a repair or replace review.

## IT-05. Desk Phone and VoIP Problems

| Owner | Director of Information Technology |
|---|---|
| Applies to | All sites with agency phones |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Restore phone service quickly. In a group home a phone is safety equipment, not an office convenience.

**Safety first.** Every site must have a working way to call 911 at all times. If the phones at a site go down, the supervisor tells all staff on shift what the backup method is, confirms each staff member has a working cell phone or the designated backup phone, notifies the on call manager, and writes down the time.

Internet phones send the address they are registered to when someone dials 911. A phone moved to a different room or site must have that address updated by IT before it is used. Never move a phone yourself.

**Try these steps first**

1. Look at the phone screen. Is it lit? What does it say? Take a photo.
2. If the screen is dark, check that the network cable is pushed in at the phone and at the wall jack. Many phones get their power through that cable.
3. Do not swap the two cables on the back of the phone. One feeds the phone and one feeds the computer.
4. Check whether other phones at the site have the same problem. If several do, report it as one site problem, not several tickets.
5. Check whether the internet is working. If the internet is down, the phones will be down. Report it once.
6. To restart the phone, unplug the network cable, wait 30 seconds, plug it back in, and wait up to three minutes. Test by calling another extension.

**Common problems**

| Problem | What to try |
|---|---|
| No dial tone | Check the handset cord at both ends, then try the speakerphone button. If the speaker works, the handset or its cord is the fault. |
| No incoming calls | Look for a Do Not Disturb or forwarding symbol on the screen and turn it off. Ask a coworker to call your extension and your direct number. |
| No outgoing calls | Write down the exact error or recording. Try an internal extension, a local number, then long distance, and note which fail. |
| Choppy or echoing audio | Test the handset, the speaker, and the headset. Note the time of day and whether it happens on every call. |
| Voicemail problems | Delete old messages if the box is full, but keep any message that documents a service or incident. For a forgotten PIN, submit a ticket. Never share a PIN. |
| Headset not working | Check that it is in the headset port, not the handset port, and press the headset button. |

**Voicemail greeting.** State your name, title, agency, and an alternate contact, and end with "If this is an emergency, please hang up and dial 911."

**Changes that need a ticket and approval.** New extensions, reassigned extensions, call group membership, call forwarding, main number and menu changes, voicemail PIN resets, access to a former employee's voicemail, and moving a phone. Staff do not contact the phone vendor directly.

Forwarding a home's main line or an on call line to a personal cell phone needs written approval from the VP of Operations and the Director of Information Technology.

**Escalate.** A site with no working phone, immediately. A home down more than one hour, tell the Chief Operating Officer. Any failed or misrouted 911 call is the most serious level of incident.

**Records.** Outage log with start and end times, the backup method used, and the address verification date for each phone.

**Response time.** A home with no phone: 15 minutes any hour, restored or a working backup within 2 hours.

## IT-08. Cybersecurity Rules for Staff

| Owner | Director of Information Technology |
|---|---|
| Applies to | Everyone |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Give staff a short list of rules that prevent most attacks.

**If something looks wrong: stop, disconnect, report.** Stop typing and do not click anything else. Unplug the network cable and turn off the wireless, but do not turn the computer off unless there is smoke, fire, or a safety hazard, because turning it off destroys information needed to find out what happened. Call [insert IT phone] right away, or [insert emergency IT number] after hours, then tell your supervisor. You will not be disciplined for reporting a mistake promptly. Not reporting is the serious problem.

**Ten rules**

1. Slow down when a message says it is urgent. Urgency is the main tool attackers use.
2. Verify any request about money or personal information by phone, using a number you already have, not a number in the message.
3. Never share your password. IT will never ask for it.
4. Never approve a 2-Step Verification prompt you did not start. If prompts keep coming, someone has your password. Deny them and call IT.
5. Hover over a link before you click it. If the address does not match the text, do not click.
6. Do not open an attachment you were not expecting. Call the sender first.
7. Lock your screen every time you walk away.
8. Install updates when prompted.
9. Report anything suspicious within one hour.
10. When you are not sure, ask. A call to IT costs nothing.

**What to watch for**

| Threat | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Phishing | A message that creates urgency, asks you to sign in, or asks for money or information |
| Executive impersonation | A message that appears to come from the CEO or COO, often from an outside address, asking for gift cards, a wire, or employee data |
| Vendor fraud | A vendor writes that their bank account has changed |
| Malware or ransomware | Sudden slowness, programs starting on their own, security software turned off, files renamed or unreadable, a note demanding payment |
| Social engineering | A caller claiming to be from IT, a vendor, or a state agency, asking you to install something or let them in |

No executive at Spectrum for Living will ever ask you by email or text to buy gift cards, send a wire transfer, or send employee payroll information. Any such message is fraud.

**If you get a suspicious email.** Do not click, reply, open attachments, or forward it to coworkers. Report it with the Gmail three dot menu, Report phishing, and forward it to [insert phishing address]. If it involves money or appears to come from an executive, also call [insert IT phone] and tell the Director of Finance. Full steps are on QR-2.

**If you already clicked or entered your password.** Call [insert IT phone] immediately. Do not send an email. Disconnect from the network. Do not change your own password first, because IT will reset it and sign the attacker out at the same time. Tell your supervisor.

**If you think a computer has a virus or ransomware.** Stop using it, disconnect it, do not turn it off, photograph the screen, and call [insert IT phone]. Tell coworkers not to use shared drives until IT says it is safe. Full steps are on QR-3.

**Lost or stolen device and disposal.** Call IT immediately, day or night, and tell your supervisor. File a police report if it was stolen. IT will lock and erase the device remotely. Shred confidential paper in a locked container, and never throw away, sell, or donate an agency device yourself.

## IT-09. Reporting a Security or Privacy Incident

| Owner | Director of Information Technology, with VP Compliance |
|---|---|
| Applies to | Everyone |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Make sure every security or privacy problem reaches the right people quickly and is handled the same way every time.

**What to report.** Any suspected or actual exposure of information about a person we support, an employee, a donor, or agency finances. This includes email sent to the wrong person, a lost document or device, a file shared improperly, an unlocked screen seen by a visitor, a suspected virus, a suspected account break in, a payment fraud attempt, and any unauthorized look at records.

**How to report.** Call [insert IT phone] right away, within one hour of noticing. After hours call [insert emergency IT number]. Tell your supervisor and complete Form E the same day. Do not delete anything, because the emails, files, and device are the record of what happened. Do not discuss the event outside the agency or on social media.

**Levels**

| Level | Description | Who is told |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Critical | Confirmed breach, active ransomware, agency wide outage, money sent to a fraudster, health information exposed | CEO, COO, Compliance, Finance, legal counsel, insurance carrier, Board Chair |
| 2. High | Confirmed account break in, stolen device with health information, health information sent outside the agency | COO, Director of IT, Compliance, and Finance if money is involved |
| 3. Moderate | Contained event with limited exposure, such as a phishing click with no password entered | Director of IT, and Compliance if health information is involved |
| 4. Low | No exposure, such as a phishing message reported and not clicked | Director of IT |

The Director of Information Technology assigns the level. Anyone may raise it. When in doubt, start higher.

**What IT does.** Confirms the report within 30 minutes and assigns a level. Saves the evidence before changing anything. Contains the problem by isolating devices, disabling accounts, and signing out active sessions. Notifies the people listed above. Determines what information and how many people are affected. Removes the cause, resets affected passwords, and restores from a clean backup if needed. Records everything in the incident log.

**Who decides what**

1. The VP Compliance decides whether the event is a reportable breach under HIPAA or New Jersey law. IT does not make that decision, and neither does the person who reported it.
2. The Director of Finance notifies the cyber insurance carrier. Most policies require notice within a set number of hours and require the use of the carrier's approved vendors. Calling late can void coverage.
3. The Chief Executive Officer and legal counsel handle any contact with law enforcement, the media, or a regulator.
4. No one pays a ransom or communicates with an attacker without the Chief Executive Officer, legal counsel, and the insurance carrier.

A cybersecurity incident does not replace an Unusual Incident Report, and a UIR does not replace this report. If an event involves both, file both.

**Review after the event.** For level 1 and level 2, the Chief Operating Officer holds a review within ten business days covering what happened, how it was found, what worked, what was slow, what control would have prevented it, and what will change. The review examines the process, not the individual. Corrective actions are assigned with names and dates.

**Records.** Incident reports are kept 7 years, and at least 6 years from resolution for anything involving health information.

## IT-10. Payment and Banking Change Verification

| Owner | Director of Finance |
|---|---|
| Applies to | Finance, billing, purchasing, executives, and anyone who can request or approve a payment |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Prevent the most likely way this agency would lose money to an attacker.

**The rule.** No change to how, where, or to whom money is sent is ever made based on an email, letter, fax, text message, or incoming phone call. Verification is always an outgoing call, by voice, to a number already in the vendor file, to a person whose name we already have.

**When this applies.** Vendor bank account or remittance changes, new vendor setup, wire transfers, ACH changes, employee direct deposit changes, payroll data requests, gift card requests, unusual or duplicate invoices, changes to a payment address, and any request to skip normal approval.

**Steps**

1. Stop. Do not process the request and do not forward it to accounting for action.
2. Complete Form H.
3. Find the vendor's phone number in the existing vendor file, the signed contract, or an invoice that predates this request. Never use a number from the request itself, its signature block, or an attachment.
4. Call that number and speak with a person. Voicemail is not verification. An email reply is not verification.
5. Confirm the exact new account and routing numbers, the effective date, and who at the vendor authorized the change.
6. Write on Form H who you called, at what number, where that number came from, who you spoke with, what they confirmed, and the date and time.
7. Get a second approval. Director of Finance below [insert amount]. Director of Finance and Chief Operating Officer above it. Add the Chief Executive Officer above [insert amount].
8. Wait one business day before the first payment on new banking details.
9. Send a confirmation notice to the vendor contact you knew before this request arrived. If the request was fraudulent, the real vendor will tell you.
10. Process the payment.

**Stop and escalate before processing if you see any of these**

1. Pressure to act today.
2. A request to keep it confidential.
3. A claim that the Chief Executive Officer or Chief Operating Officer already approved it.
4. A sender address slightly different from the vendor's usual address.
5. A reply address that differs from the sender address.
6. A contact person you have never dealt with before.
7. A request that arrives just before a weekend, a holiday, or a known executive absence.

**Gift cards.** Spectrum for Living does not buy gift cards in response to an email, text, or chat request from any executive, for any reason. Any such request is fraud. Report it and call the named executive directly.

**Payroll and tax data.** Requests for employee payroll, tax, or direct deposit information are verified in person or by voice with the named requester before anything is sent. Employee direct deposit changes are verified with the employee by Human Resources or Payroll.

**If money has already been sent**

1. Call the bank immediately and ask for a recall. The first few hours matter most.
2. Tell the Director of Finance and the Chief Operating Officer.
3. Call the cyber insurance carrier before taking other steps.
4. Report it at ic3.gov and to local police.
5. Keep every email. Do not delete anything.
6. Tell legal counsel, and follow SOP IT-09 for the mailboxes involved.

**Records.** Form H and supporting documents are kept 7 years. Finance audits a sample of banking changes each quarter to confirm the form was used.

## IT-15. Artificial Intelligence Use

| Owner | Chief Operating Officer, with Director of Information Technology |
|---|---|
| Applies to | Everyone, on any device and any account |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Let staff use AI tools to save time, while keeping private information out of them and keeping decisions about people with people.

**The governing rule.** AI may help you draft, summarize, and organize. It may never decide anything about a person. No private information about a real person goes into it.

**You may use an approved AI tool to** draft and improve emails, memos, and reports; outline plans, checklists, and templates; summarize notes you took yourself; draft job descriptions, contractor scopes of work, and facilities procedures; draft grant narratives; rewrite material in plain language; summarize published regulations, as long as you check every citation against the actual rule; and draft internal notices and agendas.

**Never enter any of the following into any AI tool**

1. Any information about a person we support. That includes names, initials, dates, site names, diagnoses, behavior plans, incident details, and medical information.
2. Any description detailed enough to identify a person. In a small service area, "the man at our Closter facility who uses a wheelchair and was recently hospitalized" identifies someone even with no name attached.
3. Employee personnel information, including reviews, discipline, medical or leave information, and pay.
4. Social Security numbers, dates of birth, or license numbers.
5. Bank account numbers, routing numbers, or credit card information.
6. Passwords or security settings.
7. Donor information.
8. Unpublished financial statements, board materials, or legal strategy.

**Never use AI to decide**

1. Anything clinical, including assessment, diagnosis, treatment, medication, or behavior support.
2. Hiring, discipline, termination, promotion, or pay.
3. Eligibility, benefits, or service authorization for a person.
4. Housing placement, transfer, roommate matching, or discharge.
5. Anything about a person's rights, restrictions, or guardianship.
6. The final content of a regulatory submission, a Unusual Incident Report, or a survey response.

**Also not permitted.** Using a personal AI account for agency work. Using a tool that is not on the approved list at [insert location of list]. Turning on an AI feature inside software the agency already uses, including meeting transcription and email summarizing, without IT approval. Recording or transcribing a meeting with an AI tool without the consent of everyone present and IT approval. Publishing anything an AI tool produced without checking it.

**Before you use AI, check five things**

1. Is this tool on the approved list?
2. Am I signed in with my Spectrum for Living account?
3. Could anything I am about to enter identify a real person?
4. Is this a decision about a person? If yes, stop.
5. Can I check every fact it gives me?

**After it answers.** Open every citation, because AI invents regulation numbers, statistics, and quotations that look real. Check every number against its actual source. Read for language that is dismissive of people with disabilities or that takes away a person's role in their own life. You are responsible for what you send. "The AI wrote it" is not an explanation.

**Report a problem.** Tell IT if an AI tool produced something wrong that was nearly used, produced biased or demeaning language, or if information was entered by mistake. If personal information was entered, call [insert IT phone] immediately. No one is disciplined for reporting an AI mistake in good faith.

This SOP requires review by Human Resources, Compliance, Clinical, and legal counsel before adoption.

## IT-16. Approving New Software and AI Tools

1. Do not discuss a person we support or an employee in a hallway, waiting area, elevator, or vehicle where others can hear.
2. Do not use speakerphone for these conversations where others are present.

**Protecting paper**

1. Keep confidential and restricted paper in locked storage.
2. Do not leave it on desks overnight, in vehicles, or at home beyond the task.
3. Shred it in a locked container.

**Photographs and video of people we support** require authorization and consent through the program consent process. Never take them on a personal device. Never post them without documented consent.

## IT-20. Backups and System Recovery

| Owner | Director of Information Technology |
|---|---|
| Applies to | All agency information and systems |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Make sure any file, mailbox, or system can be brought back, and prove it regularly. A backup that has never been restored is a belief, not a protection.

**Points to understand**

Google protects against its own equipment failing. It does not protect against a person or an attacker deleting or encrypting our information. Google Workspace needs its own backup. This is a funding decision for leadership.

**Steps**

1. Agree with each system owner how much information the agency can afford to lose, measured in time, and how long the agency can operate without that system. Proposals below require approval.

| System | Most information we can lose | Longest we can be without it |
|---|---|---|
| Care documentation system | 1 hour | 4 hours |
| Medicaid billing | 4 hours | 8 hours |
| Payroll and timekeeping | 4 hours | 8 hours |
| Google Workspace, email and files | 4 hours | 8 hours |
| Accounting | 24 hours | 24 hours |
| Ticket system | 24 hours | 24 hours |
| Donor database | 24 hours | 48 hours |

2. Back up on a schedule that meets those numbers.
3. Keep at least one copy off site that cannot be changed or deleted, including by someone with administrator access. This is the specific protection that survives ransomware, because attackers go after backups first.
4. Check backup jobs every day. Fix a failed job within one business day. Report a job that fails twice.
5. Test a restore every quarter. Restore a single file, a full mailbox, and one full system. Write down what was restored, how long it took, and whether it was usable.
6. Run a recovery exercise once a year simulating the loss of a major system, and write a short report on what worked and what did not.
7. Check backups after any major change, such as a system migration or a new application.
8. Report every month on job success rate, failures, and restore tests completed.
9. Confirm that the agency meets the backup requirements in the cyber insurance policy and keep that documentation.

**Escalate.** A failed restore test goes to the Chief Operating Officer immediately and is treated as a serious risk. A backup failure not resolved in 48 hours goes to the Chief Operating Officer. If the agreed recovery times cannot be met with current spending, the Chief Operating Officer makes a written decision to fund it or to accept the risk.

**Records.** Daily job logs are kept one year. Restore test records are kept three years. Recovery exercise reports are kept 7 years.

## IT-24. Security Training and Phishing Tests

| Owner | Director of Information Technology, with Human Resources |
|---|---|
| Applies to | Everyone with an agency account |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Build and measure the one defense attackers actually aim at, which is people, and do it in a way that makes staff more likely to report rather than less.

**Steps**

1. Assign orientation training before the account is turned on.
2. Assign security awareness training within 30 days of hire and once a year after that.
3. Send test phishing messages [monthly for higher risk roles, quarterly for all staff].
4. Build the tests around what actually threatens this agency: vendor banking changes, executive impersonation, payroll redirection, messages that appear to come from a state agency, and shared file links.
5. Measure two numbers. The click rate is how many people clicked. The report rate is how many people reported it. **The report rate matters more.** An agency where people occasionally click but report within minutes is safer than one where no one clicks and no one reports.
6. When someone clicks, send a short explanation of what the warning signs were. Do not discipline.
7. If someone clicks three times in twelve months, the supervisor and IT have a supportive conversation, assign targeted training, and consider whether the person's workload or workstation is part of the problem. Discipline is considered only for refusing to complete required training, not for clicking.
8. Never build a test message around bonuses, layoffs, benefits changes, or family emergencies. Those work, and they destroy the trust that makes people report.
9. Add role specific content: payment fraud for Finance and billing, impersonation for executives and assistants, supporting an employee who reports for supervisors.
10. Use real incidents from the agency, with names removed, in training within 30 days of the event. That is the most effective material available.
11. Provide training in accessible formats and in translation where needed. Training a person cannot use is not training.

**Yearly training outline, about 45 minutes**

| Section | Content |
|---|---|
| Why this matters here | What we hold, what an incident costs in dollars and in service disruption |
| Phishing and fraud | Real examples, how to check a link, how to report |
| Passwords and 2-Step Verification | Passphrases, never sharing, prompts you did not start |
| Money and information requests | The verification rule, gift card fraud, vendor banking changes |
| Devices and physical security | Screen locks, lost devices, USB drives, printed material, shredding |
| Working remotely | Public wireless, hotspots, home office privacy, vehicles |
| Artificial intelligence | What may and may not be entered |
| Reporting | Stop, disconnect, report, and why honest reporting is never punished |
| Knowledge check | Ten questions, 80 percent to pass, retakes allowed |

**Escalate.** A department below 90 percent completion at 45 days goes to the director. A click rate above 10 percent or a report rate below 40 percent goes to the Chief Operating Officer and the program is redesigned.

**Records.** Completion records are kept 7 years after separation. Test results are kept three years.

## IT-26. Technology Budget and Equipment Replacement

| Owner | Chief Operating Officer, with Director of Information Technology and Director of Finance |
|---|---|
| Applies to | All technology spending |
| Effective | [Insert date] |

**Purpose.** Replace equipment on a plan instead of in an emergency, which costs less and disrupts less.

**Steps**

1. Build a five year replacement schedule from the actual equipment inventory, using the expected life in SOP IT-17. Count the devices by type and purchase year to get the number due each year and the cost.
2. Level the schedule. If one year carries far more replacements than the others, move some purchases forward or back a year. A large one year spike is usually left over from a past bulk purchase and will repeat every cycle unless it is broken up.
3. Build the annual budget in four parts: ongoing costs such as the managed IT provider, licenses, internet, and cloud services; scheduled replacements; projects; and a contingency of 5 to 10 percent.
4. Report actual spending against budget every month, with the reason for any variance.
5. Report the cost of unused software licenses every quarter and put those savings back into the technology budget rather than giving them up. This funds improvements without new money.
6. Evaluate every significant purchase on four things before features: what it connects to, the total cost over three years, what it would cost to leave, and whether we can get our information out. At this size a platform choice is a commitment of five to ten years, and the cost of leaving is usually larger than the purchase price.
7. Report every year how many devices are past their replacement age and what percentage that is. If that percentage rises year over year, the agency is building up a deferred cost, and the report should say so plainly.
8. Use the repeat problem reports from the ticket system. A device that has generated three or more tickets already has a documented case for replacement.
9. Coordinate with Facilities and Fleet capital planning. Because tickets, equipment, buildings, and vehicles all sit in the same reporting line, they can be forecast in one view.
10. Work with the Director of Development on any grant opportunity for technology that improves service quality, accessibility, or efficiency.

**Escalate.** More than 15 percent of devices past replacement age, a budget variance over 10 percent, an emergency replacement over [insert amount], or any platform decision where the cost of leaving has not been written down.

**Records.** The replacement plan, budget submissions, variance reports, and cost analyses for major purchases are kept 7 years.

**Steps**

1. An employee who needs an accommodation requests it through Human Resources. Human Resources runs the process and decides the accommodation. This is a Human Resources process under the Americans with Disabilities Act and the New Jersey Law Against Discrimination, and this SOP covers only the technology part of it.
2. Human Resources gives IT the functional requirement. An employee is never asked to give IT a diagnosis or medical information.
3. IT identifies the options, the cost, and how long it will take, within five business days.
4. IT puts the approved accommodation in place within ten business days and supports it afterward. An approved accommodation delayed in purchasing is a denied accommodation in practice.
5. Check accessibility before buying any new software or device: keyboard navigation, screen reader compatibility, captioning, contrast, text size, and a vendor accessibility statement. Record the result. If a product falls short and is still chosen, write down what will be done instead. Checking this at purchase costs nothing. Fixing it later is expensive.
6. Make training accessible: captioned video, alternative formats, plain language, and translation where needed.
7. Make staff documents accessible: clear headings, alternative text on images, readable type, and plain language. That includes the quick reference guides in this manual.
8. Meet [WCAG 2.1 AA] on the website, check it once a year, and track the fixes.
9. Offer families and people we support plain language, large print, translation, and alternative formats. Do not rely on a single channel that leaves out people without internet access.
10. Equipment used by people we support follows program and clinical policy. IT supports the technical environment around it and handles those tickets under the accessibility category.
11. Give any ticket involving an accommodation or a person's assistive equipment a higher priority than the same request would otherwise receive.

**Escalate.** An accommodation not in place within ten business days of approval goes to the Chief Human Resources Officer and the Chief Operating Officer. An employee blocked by an inaccessible system needs a temporary alternative the same week.

**Records.** Purchasing accessibility checks are kept for the life of the product plus 7 years. Human Resources keeps accommodation records. IT holds no medical information.

## QR-2. How to Spot and Report a Phishing Email

**Warning signs**

1. The message says it is urgent or has a deadline today.
2. It asks for money, gift cards, a banking change, or payroll information.
3. It asks you to sign in through a link.
4. The sender's address does not match the name shown, or is slightly misspelled.
5. There is an external sender banner on a message that claims to be from a coworker.
6. There is an attachment you were not expecting.
7. It says to keep the request confidential or not to call.
8. The sender says they are in a meeting and cannot take calls.
9. A new phone number claims to be an executive.
10. Hovering over the link shows an address that does not match the text.

**What to do**

1. Do not click, reply, or open the attachment.
2. Do not forward it to coworkers to ask what they think.
3. In Gmail, click the three dots at the top right of the message and choose Report phishing.
4. Forward it to [insert phishing address].
5. If it involves money or appears to come from an executive, also call [insert IT phone] and tell the Director of Finance.
6. Wait for IT to confirm before you delete it.

**If you already clicked or typed your password**

1. Call [insert IT phone] immediately. Do not send an email.
2. Unplug the network cable and turn off the wireless.
3. Do not change your password yourself. IT will reset it and sign the attacker out at the same time.
4. Tell your supervisor.
5. You will not be in trouble. Reporting quickly is what protects everyone.

No executive at Spectrum for Living will ever ask you by email or text to buy gift cards, send a wire transfer, or send employee payroll information.

## QR-3. What to Do If a Computer May Have a Virus

**Signs.** Files renamed or will not open. A note on the screen demanding payment. Sudden extreme slowness. Programs opening by themselves. Security software turned off. Network drives suddenly failing.

**Do these in order**

1. Stop typing. Do not click anything else.
2. Unplug the network cable and turn off the wireless.
3. Do not turn the computer off, unless there is smoke, fire, or a safety hazard. Turning it off destroys information needed to find out what happened.
4. Take a photo of the screen with your phone.
5. Call [insert IT phone]. After hours call [insert emergency IT number]. Do not send an email, because email may be affected.
6. Tell your supervisor and the site lead.
7. Tell coworkers at the site out loud not to use shared drives until IT says it is safe.
8. Tell IT right away if files on a shared drive are affected. That means it is spreading and changes what IT has to do.

**Do not**

1. Run a virus scan.
2. Delete files.
3. Try to clean it up.
4. Plug in a USB drive or a phone.

**If the computer is needed for care documentation**, tell IT so a replacement is prioritized. Do not keep using the affected computer.

## QR-6. Using Artificial Intelligence Safely

**You may use an approved AI tool to** draft and improve emails, outline reports and plans, summarize notes you took yourself, draft job descriptions and contractor scopes, simplify wording, and draft checklists and templates.

**Never enter any of this into an AI tool**

1. Anything about a person we support. No names, no initials, no dates, no site names, no descriptions, no diagnoses, no incidents.
2. Employee personnel information, including reviews, discipline, medical information, and pay.
3. Social Security numbers, bank account numbers, or passwords.

**Never use AI to decide** anything about a person's services, housing, health, benefits, job, discipline, or money.

**Never use** a personal AI account, or any tool that is not on the approved list.

**Before you type, check five things**

1. Is this tool on the approved list?
2. Am I signed in with my Spectrum for Living account?
3. Could anything I am entering identify a real person?
4. Is this a decision about a person? If yes, stop.
5. Can I check every fact it gives back?

**After it answers**

1. Open every citation. AI invents regulation numbers, statistics, and quotations that look real.
2. Check every number against its real source.
3. Read for language that is dismissive of people with disabilities.
4. You are responsible for what you send. "The AI wrote it" is not an explanation.

**Made a mistake?** Tell IT at [insert email]. If you entered personal information, call [insert IT phone] right away. You will not be in trouble for reporting it.

## QR-7. Email Checklist

**Before you send, check eight things**

1. Are all the addresses correct? Did Gmail suggest the wrong person?
2. Does everyone on Reply All really need this?
3. Does this contain health, personnel, donor, or financial information? If so, is this the right way to send it?
4. Did I attach the right file? Did I check for extra tabs or rows?
5. If I sent a link, is it limited to the right people?
6. Does the subject line say the topic and what I need?
7. Would I be comfortable if a surveyor, an attorney, a funder, or the Board read this?
8. Does this ask for or approve a payment or an access change? If so, has it been verified by phone?

**Quick rules**

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Use your Spectrum for Living account for agency work | Use a personal email account |
| Put the request in the first two sentences | Bury it at the bottom |
| Use CC openly and say why | Use BCC to copy someone secretly |
| Send a Drive link limited to named people | Attach files with private information |
| Send media, legal, and regulator mail to the right office right away | Answer a reporter, attorney, or regulator yourself |

## 5.5 Suggested Order of Rollout

Adopting all of this at once will not work. Start with the parts that help staff first, then the parts that reduce the most risk.

| Stage | Weeks | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 to 4 | Site list, equipment inventory, and a current picture of shared sign ins, 2-Step Verification, and backups |
| 2 | 3 to 8 | The two Google Forms, email rules, password help, and the printed quick reference guides |
| 3 | 6 to 14 | Cybersecurity rules, incident reporting, payment verification, access removal, backups, and training |
| 4 | 10 to 18 | Phone and internet outage procedures, downtime packets, and the first drill |
| 5 | 14 to 26 | AI use, software approval, equipment inventory, vendor oversight, change control, and budget planning |
| 6 | 20 to 30 | Physical security, retention, website and social media, accessibility, and the monthly report |

Stage 2 comes before stage 3 on purpose. Staff should see that getting help became easier before they are asked to follow new rules.

## 5.6 Assumptions and Open Items

These assumptions are stated so they can be corrected.

1. Spectrum for Living uses Gmail and Google Workspace.
2. An outside managed IT provider handles daily support.
3. IT tickets and maintenance tickets will be submitted through two Google Forms.
4. Residential sites operate 24 hours a day and depend on network access for care documentation.
5. HIPAA applies to the agency.
6. Cyber liability insurance is in place, and its requirements are not yet reflected in this manual.
7. Human Resources, Compliance, and Clinical are separate departments whose agreement is required for the sections that involve them.

These items are known gaps.

1. No baseline numbers exist yet, so every target in this manual is a proposal.
2. The managed IT provider contract terms are not reflected here. The response times may be more than the contract requires.
3. The list of which job gets which access does not exist yet and cannot be written without each system owner.
4. Shared and generic sign ins at 24 hour sites are likely common. The size of that problem is not known.
5. The paper documentation procedures referred to in IT-29 must be written by Clinical and Nursing.
6. Retention periods have not been confirmed by legal counsel.
7. Cyber insurance requirements may be stricter than this manual.
8. The physical locations of network equipment at residential sites have not been surveyed.
9. AI features already turned on inside existing software have not been inventoried.
10. The accessibility of current systems has not been assessed.
